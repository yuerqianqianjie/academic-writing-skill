# Figures, Tables, Captions, and Legends

## Use this reference when

The user is writing or checking figure/table citations, captions, legends, panel descriptions, visual definitions, table formatting, or graphical abstracts.

## Integrate visuals with the Results prose

The Results source says to use visuals to illustrate points in the text, describe the relationship revealed by the data, and avoid making the reader interpret the figure alone. Prefer:

`The abundances of species A and B were inversely related (Figure 4).`

to a sentence that only says the figure contains a relationship. Use concise active references such as `Figure 2 shows ...` where appropriate. Capitalize `Figure` and `Table` when paired with a number; follow the target journal for abbreviations such as `Fig.`.

## Figure legend structure

The source usually distinguishes a caption (the identifying headline, often below the visual) from a legend (the explanatory sentences after it), while warning that some journals use the terms interchangeably. Check the target journal. A well-formed legend should support the figure independently so that readers can understand the visual without returning to Results or Methods.

The captions/legends source describes four possible components:

1. **Title:** identifies the figure and may state the analysis type, result, or major finding.
2. **Methods:** brief design/technique information when allowed by the journal.
3. **Results:** include when the title does not already state the result or when panels show several results.
4. **Definitions/descriptions:** explain symbols, colors, lines, abbreviations, scale bars, error bars, statistics, axes, and other non-intuitive features.

For multipart figures, describe each panel by its label and include the information needed to understand it without searching for unstated conventions.

## Tables and graphics

Source-supported checks include editable table text rather than images, consecutive numbering and explicit in-text mention, notes below the table body, no duplicate presentation of results, and the relevant journal's rules for lines, shading, legends, LaTeX, footnotes, and placement. Figures/schemes may have source-specific requirements for captions, in-line placement, order of mention, file type, resolution, and full-word `Figure` citations.

## Journal variation

The source preserves differences such as Science legends under 200 words, Nature legends under 300 words and no method details when a Methods section exists, PLOS ONE's succinct key-message guidance, and separate table rules for Catalysis Today, Advanced Functional Materials, and Research in Astronomy and Astrophysics. Apply only the rule attached to the relevant journal or source context.

## Diagnostic questions

- Does the prose state the data relationship rather than merely point to a visual?
- Is the visual mentioned in the required numerical order?
- Can a reader understand panels, symbols, abbreviations, error bars, and statistics?
- Is the legend too detailed or too sparse for the target journal?
- Does the table duplicate prose or use an image where editable text is required?
- Are caption/legend terms used consistently with the manuscript?
