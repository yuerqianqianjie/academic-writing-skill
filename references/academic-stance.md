# Academic Stance and Hedging

## Use this reference when

The user asks whether a claim is too strong or too weak, how to express certainty, how to hedge an interpretation, how to maintain a neutral stance, or how to choose active/passive voice without changing authorial responsibility.

## Calibrate commitment

First classify the proposition as an observation, established background, source report, interpretation, possible explanation, implication, or recommendation. Then ask how directly the evidence supports it. The wording should represent that relationship:

- direct observation may use a clear reporting verb when the data justify it;
- interpretation or mechanism may require `may`, `could`, `appear`, `seem`, `suggest`, or similar forms;
- implications and recommendations should not be presented as results unless the study actually tested them.

The source lists certainty verbs such as `show`, `demonstrate`, `reveal`, `make clear`, `discover`, and `illustrate`, and epistemic/hedging verbs such as `appear`, `seem`, `indicate`, `imply`, `predict`, `speculate`, `suggest`, and `suspect`. Select by evidence and function, not by perceived sophistication.

## Source-supported hedge types

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Modals | `could`, `may`, `might` |
| Frequency adverbs | `often`, `sometimes`, `usually` |
| Possibility adverbs | `possibly`, `probably`, `approximately`, `presumably` |
| Possibility adjectives/nouns | `likely`, `possible`, `probable`, `possibility`, `assumption` |
| Epistemic verbs | `appear`, `seem`, `believe`, `estimate`, `indicate`, `imply`, `suggest`, `suspect` |

Hedging is not ornamental politeness. In the supplied Discussion material it protects the writer from overcommitment, recognizes alternatives, and presents reasons, interpretations, and implications as possible or probable.

## Stance diagnostic

- Has a tentative inference been made categorical?
- Has a factual result been weakened so much that the finding is unclear?
- Does `suggest`, `indicate`, or `demonstrate` match the analysis and evidence?
- Is the writer distinguishing what the data show from what the writer infers?
- Does the wording acknowledge relevant limitations or alternative explanations?
- Does passive voice appropriately foreground the action, or does it conceal who made a choice that should be attributed?

## Voice and stance

Voice is an information-focus choice, not a universal marker of academic style. Methods may use passive voice to foreground what was done, but the source also lists active subjects such as `we`, `this study`, and `this work`. In Discussion, active wording may be useful when the writer needs to own an interpretation; neutral formulations may be useful when the data are the focus. Do not remove agency merely to sound formal.
