# Academic Vocabulary and Phraseology

## Use this reference when

The user asks for reporting verbs, research verbs, academic collocations, concise wording, or discipline-appropriate phraseology after the rhetorical function and evidence are understood.

## Function before phrase

Choose words according to what the sentence is doing: report, compare, evaluate, propose, justify, interpret, qualify, or conclude. A source phrase is a possible linguistic realization, not a fixed template. Do not use a stronger verb simply because it sounds more academic.

## Reporting verbs from the source

The source records field tendencies rather than rules:

- Biology: `describe`, `find`, `report`, `show`, `suggest`, `observe`.
- Physics: `develop`, `report`, `study`, `find`, `expand`.
- Electrical engineering: `propose`, `use`, `describe`, `show`, `publish`, `develop`.
- Mechanical engineering: `describe`, `show`, `report`, `discuss`, `give`.
- Marketing and applied linguistics: `suggest`, `argue`, `find`, `demonstrate`, `propose`, `explain`.
- Sociology and philosophy: `argue`, `suggest`, `describe`, `note`, `analyze`, `claim`, `point out`.

Use the discipline list only as a prompt for checking local convention. Confirm the verb against the cited study and target journal.

## Methods verbs and purpose patterns

The Methods source lists `adopt`, `apply`, `develop`, `establish`, `employ`, `estimate`, `measure`, `select`, `use`, and `utilize`. It also gives purpose patterns beginning `In order to ...`, `To ...`, `For the purpose of ...`, `Our aim was ...`, and `This choice was aimed at ...`. Preserve the source's degree of specificity; do not complete an ellipsis with invented content.

## Readability and concision

The Readability source recommends concrete words when precision matters, verbs instead of heavy noun strings, one verb instead of unnecessary verb+noun combinations, and shorter expressions when they remain unambiguous. It also warns against:

- redundant words and near-synonym strings;
- generic + specific constructions;
- unnecessary `it is` openings and authorial commentary;
- changing a key technical term merely to avoid repetition (the source calls this “synonymomania”/“monologophobia” in its discussion of reference).

Concision means the fewest words that make the meaning fully clear, not the fewest words at any cost.
