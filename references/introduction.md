# Introduction

## Use this reference when

The user is planning, diagnosing, teaching, revising, or drafting an Introduction, or when a paragraph must establish a research area, review prior work, identify a gap, and present the current study.

## Source-derived purpose and rhetorical progression

The source describes the Introduction as a preview: give enough background for readers to understand why the questions are asked, the context, and why hypotheses or predictions are reasonable. Its broad progression is **general → specific**.

| Move | Function | Possible steps |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Introduce the research area | Establish the importance of the topic and provide necessary background. | Centrality, context, problem, or established knowledge. |
| 2. Review previous studies | Show progress and position the current problem. | Achievements/progress; research gap. |
| 3. Present the present work | Make the current study legible and motivated. | Purpose; questions/hypotheses; definitions; brief methods; principal findings; value; article structure. |

The steps are rhetorical options, not a compulsory sentence sequence. Check whether each included sentence advances the move's function and whether the reader can see the transition from territory to gap to present work.

## Diagnostic questions

- Is the background necessary for understanding the research question, rather than a broad information dump?
- Does the literature review establish what is known and what remains unresolved?
- Is the gap specific enough to motivate this study, and is it supported by the cited literature?
- Does the present-study statement match the actual questions, hypotheses, methods, and results?
- Is the contribution or value stated at a level supported by the evidence?
- Does the paragraph or section move from context toward the study without jumping to unsupported conclusions?

## Source-supported language functions

Use these as optional realizations of a rhetorical function, not as templates:

- Centrality: `X is fundamental to ...`; `X is a major area of interest within ...`
- Prior work: `Previous/prior studies have investigated/found ...`; `Several studies have suggested/shown that ...`
- Gap: `However, few studies have focused on ...`; `It is still unclear whether ...`
- Present work: `The present study examines ...`; `The purposes of this paper are ...`

If a stronger centrality or gap claim would require evidence that is not supplied, soften or flag the missing support rather than inserting a stronger phrase.

## Tense guidance from the source

| Function | Common source-supported tense |
|---|---|
| General background | Present simple; sometimes present perfect. |
| Prior progress or gap over time | Present perfect; present simple is also used for a current gap. |
| Purpose, questions, hypotheses, definitions, value, structure | Present simple. |
| Brief methods summary | Past simple. |
| Principal outcomes | Present or past, depending on the rhetorical focus and target convention. |

These are functional tendencies, not automatic corrections. Preserve a tense if changing it would alter the time relation or source claim.

## Introduction versus Abstract

The source distinguishes them: an Abstract is a concise, self-contained summary that includes the most important findings and conclusion; an Introduction is more detailed, establishes centrality, reviews work, identifies a gap, and presents the current work. Do not make the Introduction a longer Abstract or make the Abstract carry an extended literature review.

## Journal-sensitive variation

The supplied journal guidance is not uniform:

- Science Robotics asks for the problem, importance, detailed scientific question, enough cross-disciplinary background, and context for implications.
- IEEE includes existing literature to position novelty and importance.
- Chinese Journal of Aeronautics asks for objectives and adequate background while avoiding a detailed literature survey or results summary.

If a target journal is named, follow its guidance. If not, preserve the difference and state which balance is being used; do not present one journal's Introduction pattern as universal.
