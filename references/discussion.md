# Discussion

## Use this reference when

The user is interpreting findings, comparing them with prior work, explaining possible causes, stating innovation or contribution, discussing implications and limitations, or deciding how Discussion relates to Conclusions.

## Purpose and structure

The source presents Discussion as the place to highlight the innovation of the research and justify why the paper was written. Unlike the Introduction's general-to-specific progression, it describes Discussion as moving from specific findings toward more general interpretation in a pyramid-like structure.

| Move | Function |
|---|---|
| 1 | Revisit the research purpose and methodology. |
| 2 | Revisit key results. |
| 3 | Comment on results through interpretation, evaluation, explanation, or comparison. |
| 4 | State contributions or implications. |
| 5 | State limitations. |
| 6 | Recommend future research. |

The source does not say that all six moves or one fixed order are mandatory. Use the target article design and evidence.

## Ways to begin

The source gives four options: remind readers of the goals; refer back to Introduction questions, hypotheses, or predictions; reconnect with cited literature; and briefly restate the most important results. A strong opening need not use all four.

## Distinguish the work done by each sentence

- **Result summary:** what was found; traceable to Results.
- **Interpretation:** what the result may mean.
- **Explanation:** why it may have occurred; distinguish supported mechanisms from possibilities.
- **Comparison:** how it agrees or conflicts with prior studies.
- **Contribution/implication:** what the finding adds or may matter for.
- **Limitation:** what the design or evidence cannot establish.

If a paragraph only repeats the Results, identify the missing interpretive or comparative function. If it jumps from one finding to a broad implication, identify the missing reasoning or evidence.

## Hedges and neutral stance

The source treats hedging as central to academic argument because it withholds complete commitment and presents explanations, interpretations, and implications as plausible rather than certain. Source-listed forms include:

- modals: `could`, `may`, `might`;
- frequency adverbs: `often`, `sometimes`, `usually`;
- possibility adverbs: `possibly`, `probably`, `approximately`, `presumably`;
- possibility adjectives/nouns: `likely`, `possible`, `probable`, `assumption`, `possibility`;
- epistemic verbs: `appear`, `seem`, `indicate`, `imply`, `predict`, `speculate`, `suggest`, `suspect`.

The source contrasts reasons → possible explanations, interpretations → possible interpretations, and implications → probable implications. Match the hedge to the evidence; do not weaken a well-supported factual report merely to sound cautious, and do not make a tentative inference categorical.

## Discussion and Conclusions

The source allows contributions, limitations, and future research to appear in Conclusions when that section exists, but also notes that some papers put them in Discussion and then add a later Conclusion. Treat this as a structural choice controlled by the target journal and article design. Load [conclusion.md](conclusion.md) when Conclusions are separate.
