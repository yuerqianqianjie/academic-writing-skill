# Methodology / Methods

## Use this reference when

The user is writing or reviewing Methods, Methodology, Materials and Methods, Experimental, Method Description and Validation, protocols, procedures, data analysis, reproducibility, or methodological justification.

## Purpose and three questions

The source presents Methods as a sufficiently detailed description of materials and procedures so that others in the field can follow and potentially replicate the work. Test whether the section answers:

1. How is the problem studied?
2. What is used to study it?
3. How does the researcher proceed?

Section name and placement vary by journal. Do not normalize them without checking the target journal.

## Possible organization

The source gives a seven-part framework:

1. Overview of the research method.
2. Research aims, questions, or hypotheses.
3. Subjects and/or materials.
4. Location, with a reason where relevant.
5. Procedure used to obtain the data.
6. Method limitations, with an explanation where useful.
7. Data analysis.

This is an organizing framework, not a claim that every study must include all seven parts. Reorder or omit components when the study design and journal require it, but do not omit information needed to understand or reproduce the work.

## Diagnostic checks

- Are materials, subjects, settings, instruments, software, measurements, and procedures specified at the level needed by the field?
- Can a qualified reader understand what was done, in what order, and how data were analyzed?
- Are choices or aims justified where the rationale affects interpretation or reproducibility?
- Is the description complete yet concise, clear, and logically ordered?
- Does a sentence usually describe no more than two steps, as the source recommends?
- Are limitations stated rather than concealed when they affect the method?

## Tense and voice

- Past simple: actions done before or during the experiment.
- Present simple: standard methods not invented by the researcher, or the presentation of a procedure, model, software, or device.
- Passive voice can foreground what was done: `Ethical approval was obtained ...`.
- Active subjects such as `we`, `this study`, `this paper`, and `this work` are also listed in the source.

Choose voice by information focus and clarity. The source does not require one voice throughout the section.

## Research verbs and justification

Source-listed research verbs include `adopt`, `apply`, `develop`, `establish`, `employ`, `estimate`, `measure`, `select`, `use`, and `utilize`. Use the verb whose meaning matches the actual action.

Source-supported purpose patterns include:

- `In order to validate the results, we first had to ...`
- `To provide a way of characterizing the samples, ...`
- `For the purpose of investigating ..., we ...`
- `Our aim was to ...`

These are incomplete source examples and should not be filled with invented details.

## Journal-specific rules

The supplied source keeps these rules separate:

- Nature Communications: Methods should contain what is needed for interpretation and replication, be concise, typically stay within 3,000 words unless necessary, and may link deposited step-by-step protocols.
- Advanced Materials: equations should be labeled consecutively and cited by label.
- Journal of The Mechanics And Physics of Solids: editable text, inline simple formulae where possible, solidus for small fractions, italic variables, `exp` where useful, and consecutive numbering when equations are separately displayed and referred to.
- Science Advances: display equations on separate lines and number them; the source also gives equation-editor and punctuation guidance.

Do not combine these into a universal Methods or equation rule. Use the target journal's current instructions when available.
