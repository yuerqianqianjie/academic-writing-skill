# Cohesion, Coherence, and Information Flow

## Use this reference when

The user asks why a paragraph is hard to follow, how to improve topic progression, how to connect sentences, how to organize information, or how to make a fluent passage more coherent without changing its meaning.

## Paragraph-level workflow

1. State the paragraph's single main rhetorical job.
2. Identify the sentence that establishes that job and whether its position helps the reader.
3. Mark each sentence as context, claim, evidence, explanation, contrast, consequence, or transition.
4. Check whether each sentence answers the previous sentence's need and prepares the next one.
5. Check whether key terms remain stable and whether pronouns have one obvious antecedent.
6. Remove or move sentences that perform a different job; do not add transitions to conceal a missing argument.

The section references supply different macro patterns: Introduction general → specific, Discussion specific → general, Literature Review overview → individual studies → evaluation → necessity, and Methods overview → aims → materials → location → procedure → analysis. Use the relevant pattern only when it fits the paper and field.

## Sentence information flow

The Readability source recommends making relationships easy to process on first reading:

- keep subject–verb–object relations visible;
- avoid delaying the subject or separating subject and verb with heavy parenthetical material;
- introduce a noun before using a pronoun;
- put a sentence's aim before its means when that clarifies the purpose;
- locate negation early enough that readers do not follow the wrong interpretation;
- keep related words close and put adverbs where their scope is clear;
- use punctuation, full stops, and explicit clauses when embedded information is overloaded.

## Cohesive devices

Use a transition only when it performs a real relation such as contrast, addition, cause, consequence, or qualification. The source warns against unnecessary link words and authorial commentary. When a relationship is important, state it explicitly; when it is already clear, do not add `it is worthwhile noting that`, `as a matter of fact`, or similar padding.

Repeat a key technical term when a synonym would suggest a different concept. Avoid vague `this`, `these`, `it`, `they`, `the former`, `the latter`, and generic nouns such as `factor`, `process`, or `element` when the antecedent is unclear.

## Sentence length and division

The source gives several checks—around 25 words, avoiding sentences over 35 words, and a maximum of about 30 words—rather than one absolute threshold. Treat them as warning signals. Divide a sentence when it contains overloaded coordination, repeated relative clauses, long comma chains, or several methodological steps. Do not turn an entire paper into uniform short sentences; combine when that removes redundancy and vary length when it improves flow.

## Diagnosis categories

- **Cohesion problem:** the linguistic link or reference is unclear.
- **Coherence problem:** the ideas do not form a purposeful logical progression.
- **Information-flow problem:** important information appears too late or the reader cannot identify the sentence's point.
- **Argumentation problem:** the passage lacks evidence or reasoning; a transition cannot fix it.

When revising, show whether you moved, deleted, split, or rephrased a sentence and why. Preserve technical terms and the author's intended relationships.
