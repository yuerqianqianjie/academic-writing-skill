# Literature Review

## Use this reference when

The user is reviewing, synthesizing, comparing, citing, or evaluating previous studies, or needs to make a literature-based gap and explain how the present study responds to it.

## Purpose and guiding questions

The source treats background information as a way to elaborate the achievements and limitations of other studies and relate new facts and data to them. Use these six questions to test coverage and synthesis:

1. Which influential works matter to this topic?
2. What progress has been made since them?
3. Which recent works are most relevant, and in what order should they appear?
4. What are the achievements and limitations of those works?
5. What gap follows from those limitations?
6. How does the present work intend to address the gap?

## Rhetorical structure

| Move | Function |
|---|---|
| 1. Overview | Introduce the topic and comment on the literature as a whole. |
| 2. Individual studies | Review studies and compare/contrast sources. |
| 3. Evaluation | Discuss strengths, weaknesses, conflicts, and a gap or further-study area. |
| 4. Necessity of the present study | Explain why the current study is needed. |

Do not equate a list of paper summaries with synthesis. A synthesized paragraph should make the relationship among sources visible: convergence, contrast, development, limitation, or unresolved conflict.

## Diagnostic workflow

- Mark each sentence as background, source report, comparison, evaluation, gap, or present-study necessity.
- Check whether multiple sources are grouped by an analytical point rather than only by publication order.
- Check whether evaluative language is supported by what the cited studies actually did.
- Check whether a stated gap is a gap in evidence, method, population, context, theory, or interpretation; do not guess which one the author means.
- Check whether the proposed study plausibly addresses the stated gap.

## Citation style and word order

The source presents several permissible emphases:

- Author-focused: `Blinco [1992] found that ...`
- Paper-first or similar numeric style: `In [5] Blinco found that ...`
- Finding-focused passive: `A study ... is presented by Blinco [1992].`
- Information-focused: `A greater level of persistence has been noticed in Japan [5].`

Choose the style according to whether the sentence foregrounds the researcher, the study, or the finding, and then follow the target journal's citation format. Do not treat the source examples as universally valid citation syntax.

## Tense and reporting verbs

- Present simple or present perfect generally introduces the literature review.
- Past simple is used when the publication year is in the sentence, when a specific historical study or abandoned method is described, or when an exact date is given.
- Reporting verbs should reflect the source's action and degree of commitment. The source lists high-frequency tendencies by field, not mandatory vocabularies: biology `describe/find/report/show/suggest/observe`; physics `develop/report/study/find/expand`; engineering often `propose/use/describe/show/develop`; marketing and applied linguistics often `suggest/argue/find/demonstrate/propose`; sociology and philosophy often `argue/suggest/describe/analyze/claim`.

Use the actual verb supported by the cited paper. `Show`, `demonstrate`, `argue`, and `suggest` are not interchangeable when the source's evidence or stance differs.

## Citation principles recorded in the source

The source notes citing recent work from the last 3–5 years, leading SCI journals, and “citing friendly.” The last phrase is preserved in the source without interpretation; do not invent a rule from it. Verify current citation requirements against the user's target journal or style guide.
