# Academic Argumentation

## Use this reference when

The user asks whether a paper has a clear argument, whether evidence supports a claim, how to state a research gap or contribution, how to interpret results, or why a paragraph is logically weak despite fluent English.

This file operationalizes the user's requested argumentation workflow from the supplied section materials. It does not add a new disciplinary theory or permit unsupported claims.

## Claim–evidence–reasoning audit

For each consequential sentence or paragraph, identify:

1. **Claim:** what the writer wants the reader to accept.
2. **Evidence:** data, analysis, cited study, method, comparison, or observation available in the text.
3. **Reasoning:** why that evidence supports the claim.
4. **Qualification:** what the evidence cannot establish.
5. **Rhetorical job:** background, literature synthesis, gap, purpose, result, interpretation, contribution, limitation, implication, or recommendation.

If any element is absent, distinguish “not stated” from “not true.” Ask for or mark the missing information; do not fill it in.

## Section-sensitive argument checks

| Section | Argumentation question |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Does the background lead to a supported gap and a current-study purpose? |
| Literature Review | Are studies synthesized and evaluated, or merely listed? Does the stated gap follow from the review? |
| Methods | Do the design and choices make the research question answerable and the procedure followable? |
| Results | Are observations separated from interpretations appropriate to the section arrangement? |
| Discussion | Does each interpretation or explanation follow from the result and relevant literature? |
| Conclusion | Does the general message remain within the evidence and reflect limitations? |
| Abstract | Are purpose, method, findings, and meaning consistent with the full paper? |

## Common reasoning failures to diagnose explicitly

- A gap is asserted with `few studies` or `little is known` but no literature basis is shown.
- A list of citations is called a synthesis without comparison, development, conflict, or evaluation.
- A result is restated as if it were an explanation or implication.
- Association or correlation is rewritten as causation.
- A study's limited sample or context is generalized to a wider population.
- A possible mechanism is presented as established fact.
- A contribution is named as “novel,” “important,” or “significant” without stating what changed or what evidence supports the evaluation.
- A limitation is mentioned but its effect on interpretation is not explained.
- A conclusion or recommendation introduces information not present in the Results or Discussion.

## Argumentative sequence

Use the source's rhetorical structures flexibly:

```text
establish territory → synthesize prior work → identify supported gap
→ state purpose/question → report evidence → interpret within limits
→ state contribution/implication → identify limitations/future work
```

Not every paper needs every step. The sequence is a diagnostic map, not a template.

## Revision response

When the problem is argumentation, lead with the diagnosis and evidence risk. If a rewrite is requested, preserve all facts and mark placeholders such as `[evidence needed]` or `[specify population]` instead of inventing content. Explain when a grammatical repair cannot solve the underlying reasoning problem.
