# Abstract

## Use this reference when

The user is drafting, shortening, revising, comparing, or checking a textual Abstract/Summary, including its background, purpose, methods, results, conclusions, implications, keywords, or journal-specific limits.

## Purpose and five-part structure

The source describes an Abstract as a self-contained “mini paper” judged in isolation. It helps editors assess relevance and helps readers decide whether to read the article.

| Component | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Background | Why was the project carried out? |
| Present research/purpose | What is this study about? |
| Methods | How was it done? |
| Results/findings | What was discovered? |
| Conclusions/implications/recommendations | What do the findings mean? |

Background should occupy less than 25% of the Abstract according to the source, because readers mainly need the new information; results are usually the main focus. This is source guidance, not a universal word-count law.

## Opening strategies

The source gives three options: begin with a real-world phenomenon or standard practice, a problem or uncertainty, or present-researcher action. Choose the opening that makes the paper's motivation clear without adding background the Abstract cannot support.

## Tense by function

- Background and purpose: present simple.
- Methods and results: commonly past simple; present simple can also be possible for Methods or current interpretation.
- Ongoing situations or achievements: present perfect/present perfect continuous where the time relation requires it.
- Conclusions/implications: present or present perfect for achievements; present simple for applications.

Use tense to represent the component and time relation, not as a mechanical copy-editing rule.

## Journal-specific variation

The supplied material preserves differences rather than one rule:

- Nature: referenced summary paragraph, ideally no more than 200 words, with limited numbers/abbreviations/measurements and a broad readership orientation.
- Mechanics of Materials and Nuclear Science and Techniques: concise, factual, stand-alone; avoid references and define uncommon abbreviations if essential.
- IEEE: 150–250 words, one paragraph, self-contained, no abbreviations/footnotes/references/equations, and keyword guidance.
- IEEE Electron Device Letters: up to 250 words, one paragraph, novelty and implications, and no mathematical equations or unexplained abbreviations.
- Physical Review B: subjects, conclusions, results, and for experiments the measured quantities, objects, and conditions; avoid unexplained acronyms, citations, and footnotes.

The source has limited exceptions and attribution ambiguities; use the target journal's current instructions when available and do not generalize any one limit.

## Graphical Abstract

The source treats a graphical Abstract as optional but encouraged in the Material Letters guidance. Its intended function is a concise pictorial overview, often context → method → outcome. Route detailed visual preparation to [figures-tables.md](figures-tables.md) only when needed.
