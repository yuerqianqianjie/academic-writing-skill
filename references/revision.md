# Revision and Proofreading Workflow

## Use this reference when

The user wants a paragraph, section, or whole paper revised, diagnosed, proofread, outlined, or compared, especially when several kinds of problems are mixed.

## Five-level review

Review in this order:

1. **Content:** relevance, completeness, clear claims, adequate evidence, accurate methods/results.
2. **Argumentation:** validity of reasoning, claim–evidence relation, research gap, contribution, interpretation, implications, limitations.
3. **Organization:** rhetorical purpose, paragraph structure, information order, section moves, transitions, Results–Discussion boundary.
4. **Academic discourse:** stance, hedging, certainty, authorial focus, discipline-sensitive conventions.
5. **Language:** grammar, vocabulary, collocation, sentence structure, ambiguity, concision, punctuation, and proofreading.

This order is a working safeguard, not a requirement to discuss every level in every answer. If the user's request is explicitly language-only, work at Level 5 while flagging meaning-changing risks.

## Readability-focused revision pass

Use this pass after the content, argumentation, and organization diagnosis has established what the text must say. Its criterion is first-reading processability: readers should be able to identify the sentence's point, follow the relationships among ideas, and locate important information without reconstructing the writer's intended meaning. Apply only the checks that fit the passage; readability is not a demand for the shortest possible text.

### 1. Information order and word placement

- Keep the subject, verb, direct object, and indirect object in a recognizable order, and keep related words close. Do not delay the subject or separate the subject and verb with long parenthetical material unless the insertion genuinely improves the flow.
- Introduce a noun before using a pronoun that refers forward. Put negations early enough to prevent the reader from initially following the wrong interpretation.
- Put the aim before the means when that makes the purpose easier to understand. Place adverbs so that their scope is clear, keep adjectives next to the nouns they modify, and avoid unverified strings of nouns.
- If an insertion, relative clause, or long modifier interrupts the main clause, move it, rephrase it, or make it a separate sentence. A short insertion may remain when it makes the sentence clearer.

### 2. Sentence length, structure, and division

- Treat the source's checks—around 25 words, generally avoiding sentences over 35 words, and a maximum of about 30 words—as warning signals rather than hard limits. Logical load matters more than word count alone.
- Consider dividing a sentence when it contains several embedded clauses, a long comma chain, repeated `and`/`as well as`, extended `which` clauses, multiple methodological steps, a semicolon, or explanatory parentheses. Give each new sentence a complete and explicit relation; do not move a dependent linker such as `although`, `since`, or `because` to the start of a sentence that is no longer dependent.
- Combine short sentences when combination removes redundancy or makes the relation clearer. Do not turn a whole passage into uniform short sentences; occasional short sequences can emphasize an important point.
- After splitting a sentence, repeat a key technical word when that helps the reader track the topic. Repetition is preferable to ambiguity.

### 3. Concision without loss of meaning

- Use the fewest words that make the meaning completely clear, not the fewest words at any cost. Preserve necessary qualifications, technical detail, and evidence.
- Remove redundant words, near-synonym strings, generic-plus-specific announcements, pointless introductions, and authorial commentary that adds no function. Keep a transition or link word when it expresses a real contrast, addition, cause, consequence, qualification, or evidential relation.
- Prefer a precise concrete term, a shorter expression, or a verb-based construction when it remains unambiguous. Check noun-heavy phrases and unnecessary verb-plus-noun combinations such as `carry out an analysis` when `analyze` expresses the same meaning.
- Replace indirect `it is` openings with a direct subject, modal, or adverb only when the change preserves the intended hedge and authorial responsibility. Avoid changing voice merely to sound more formal.
- Refer to figures and tables directly and state their main message without duplicating information that is already visible in them.

### 4. Ambiguity, reference, and terminology

- Check every `it`, `this`, `these`, `they`, `them`, `one`, `the former`, and `the latter`. If more than one antecedent is possible, repeat the exact noun or name the document location rather than making the reader infer it.
- Keep key technical terms stable. Do not replace them with synonyms merely to avoid repetition; use a synonym only when a real distinction is intended or when the source term is not yet explained to the intended readers.
- Replace vague generic words such as `factor`, `process`, `element`, or `feature` when the concrete referent is available. Check whether a relative pronoun refers to the immediately preceding noun, whether a defining/non-defining clause expresses the intended scope, and whether an `-ing` phrase has a clear subject.
- Make comparisons and relations explicit when the reader could infer the wrong one, including consequence versus means (`thus` versus `by`), agreement versus contrast, and origin versus agent (`from` versus `by`).

### 5. Precision and grammatical signaling

- Use an exact number when it matters; otherwise choose a proportionate quantifier. Reconsider vague terms such as `recently`, `substantially`, `appropriate`, or `very` when different readers could assign different meanings.
- Use punctuation, hyphens, articles, conjunctions, and relative-clause punctuation to show the intended relationships. Check inclusive versus exclusive alternatives (`both ... and` / `either ... or`), pairings marked by `respectively`, and the distinction between `i.e.` and `e.g.` where relevant to the target journal.
- Check contextual typos, false friends, uncountable nouns, and article choices during proofreading; a spell-checker may not detect errors that produce a grammatical but unintended meaning.

### Source-supported revision moves

Use these as options, not templates:

| Readability problem | Possible revision move |
|---|---|
| Delayed or impersonal opening | `It is possible to do this with the new system.` → `This can be done with the new system.` |
| Unnecessary verb-plus-noun construction | `carry out an analysis` → `analyze` when the technical meaning is unchanged |
| Ambiguous forward reference | Replace an initial `it` with the noun it describes before using a later pronoun |
| Unclear figure reference | `As we can see in Fig. 1...` → `Figure 1 highlights...` when the figure itself is the information focus |

Do not apply a move mechanically. Recheck the revised sentence against the paragraph's purpose, evidence, stance, and technical terminology.

## Readability conflict and scope protocol

- A readability edit must not conceal a missing argument, change a claim, or remove information required for reproducibility. If shorter wording would change emphasis or precision, retain the necessary detail and explain the trade-off.
- The sentence-length checks and word-order preferences are source-supported warnings with exceptions, not universal grammar laws. Follow the target journal and discipline when they control terminology, section structure, voice, punctuation, or accessibility.
- When a readability problem is actually a coherence or argumentation problem, diagnose that higher-level issue first. A transition, shorter sentence, or smoother synonym cannot supply missing reasoning or evidence.
- Report whether a revision moved, split, deleted, or rephrased material when that change affects information order or reference. State any assumption needed to resolve an ambiguity.

## Diagnose before changing

Label the main issue as one or more of:

- **Grammar:** form is ungrammatical or violates a clear agreement, tense, article, or punctuation requirement.
- **Style/readability:** grammar is acceptable but wording, order, length, or redundancy obstructs processing.
- **Logic/coherence:** ideas or references do not progress clearly, or a sentence does not connect to its paragraph purpose.
- **Argumentation/evidence:** the claim exceeds the evidence, the reasoning is missing, the gap is unsupported, or an interpretation is presented as a result.

Do not call an argumentation problem a grammar problem just because a grammatical rewrite sounds smoother.

## Mode-specific behavior

- **Explain/teach:** show the rhetorical function and checks; use a small illustrative rewrite only if it teaches the point.
- **Diagnose:** report issue → evidence in the text → consequence → targeted remedy.
- **Revise/rewrite:** provide the revised text first, then a short change summary and unresolved questions.
- **Proofread:** make only language corrections requested; preserve terminology, claims, stance, and results.
- **Outline/generate:** list rhetorical functions, evidence needed, and placeholders; never invent missing facts or citations.
- **Compare:** compare alternatives by meaning, evidence, stance, coherence, discipline, and journal fit.

## Minimal-change protocol

1. Preserve all numbers, entities, citations, methods, findings, and qualification unless the user explicitly asks to change them.
2. Preserve the author's intended technical terms and distinguishable concepts.
3. Make the smallest change that resolves the diagnosed issue.
4. If the text cannot be repaired without a substantive decision, stop at the decision point and explain what is missing.
5. State any assumption used in a revision.

## Final check

Confirm that the result matches the requested task, section, discipline, and journal; that no citation or evidence was invented; that certainty was not accidentally increased; and that language improvements did not conceal a logic or argument problem.

For a readability-sensitive revision, also confirm that the main subject and verb are easy to locate, sentence divisions preserve logical relations, key terms and pronoun references are stable, necessary repetition has not been removed, and concision has not reduced precision.
